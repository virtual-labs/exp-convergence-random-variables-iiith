Convergence of Random Variables

In the realm of probability theory and statistical inference, it's common to encounter situations where we aim to estimate an unobservable random variable XXX through a sequence of approximations. Suppose we cannot observe XXX directly, but we can perform measurements or experiments to obtain estimates X1,X2,X3,…X_1, X_2, X_3, \ldotsX1​,X2​,X3​,…. Each subsequent estimate is derived from additional data or refined methodologies, with the hope that as nnn increases, XnX_nXn​ provides a more accurate approximation of XXX.

This leads us to the concept of convergence: we are interested in understanding whether and how the sequence {Xn}\{X_n\}{Xn​} approaches XXX as n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞. In probability theory, convergence isn't a singular notion but encompasses various types, each capturing a different aspect of how XnX_nXn​ may become "close" to XXX. These include:

  1. Almost Sure Convergence: XnX_nXn​ converges to XXX with probability 1.
  2. Convergence in Probability: For any ϵ>0\epsilon > 0ϵ>0, the probability that ∣Xn−X∣>ϵ|X_n - X| > \epsilon∣Xn​−X∣>ϵ approaches zero as n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞.
  3. Convergence in Distribution: The distribution functions of XnX_nXn​ converge to the distribution function of XXX at all continuity points.
  4. Mean Square Convergence: The expected value of ∣Xn−X∣2|X_n - X|^2∣Xn​−X∣2 approaches zero as n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞.

These are all different kinds of convergence. A sequence might converge in one sense but not another. Some of these convergence types are ''stronger'' than others and some are ''weaker.'' By this, we mean the following: If Type A convergence is stronger than Type B convergence, it means that Type A convergence implies Type B convergence. The below figure summarizes how these types of convergence are related. In this figure, the stronger types of convergence are on top and, as we move to the bottom, the convergence becomes weaker. For example, using the figure, we conclude that if a sequence of random variables converges in probability to a random variable XXX, then the sequence converges in distribution to XXX as well.

1. Almost Sure Convergence

Definition

A sequence of random variables {Xn}\{X_n\}{Xn​} converges almost surely to a random variable XXX if:

P(lim⁡n→∞Xn=X)=1 \begin{equation} \mathbb{P}\left( \lim_{n \to \infty} X_n = X \right) = 1 \end{equation} P(n→∞lim​Xn​=X)=1​​

This means that the sequence XnX_nXn​ converges to XXX for almost every outcome in the sample space. Almost sure convergence implies that, with probability 1, the sequence Xn(ω)X_n(\omega)Xn​(ω) approaches X(ω)X(\omega)X(ω) as n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞. It's akin to saying that the convergence happens for "almost every" individual outcome.

As n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞, Xn(ω)→1X_n(\omega) \to 1Xn​(ω)→1 for all ω∈[0,1]\omega \in [0,1]ω∈[0,1]. Therefore, XnX_nXn​ converges almost surely to 1.

5. Relationships Between Different Types of Convergence

As discussed previously, the different types of convergences are related to each other.

  1. Almost Sure Convergence ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ Convergence in Probability ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ Convergence in Distribution
  2. Convergence in Mean Square ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ Convergence in Probability ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ Convergence in Distribution
